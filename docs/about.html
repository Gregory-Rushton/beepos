About our bees

Bee Positive Apiary is a family operation. We started in 2019 with two colonies at our home in Easton and later added a second bee yard in Norton. Today we keep about 10 to 12 colonies each year, split evenly between the two locations.

Two bee yards

Honey bees usually forage within a few miles of their hive, so each yard's bees visit a different mix of trees, wildflowers and gardens. Keeping colonies in two places also spreads the risk: a bad season in one spot doesn't affect them all.

Two harvests a year

We harvest honey twice a year, in spring and in summer. Different plants bloom at different times, so the two harvests can look and taste different. Spring honey is often lighter and milder; summer honey is often darker, with a stronger flavor.

Keeping colonies healthy

The biggest threat to honey bees in New England is the varroa mite, a parasite that weakens colonies and spreads viruses among them. We treat our colonies with oxalic acid vapor. Oxalic acid occurs naturally in foods like spinach and rhubarb, and it is registered for use in beehives.

We strive to keep our bees healthy in an ethical and sustainable manner, and we learn a great deal from local beekeeping clubs and other beekeepers.
